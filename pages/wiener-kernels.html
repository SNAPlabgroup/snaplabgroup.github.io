Wiener kernels to characterize temporal coding properties of neurons

How reverse correlation turns broadband noise and a spike train into a description of what a neuron listens for — first the waveform, then its envelope.

A healthy cochlea splits a sound into a bank of narrowly tuned frequency channels. The output of any one channel has two parts. There is a carrier oscillating near the characteristic frequency (CF) of that cochlear place, called temporal fine structure (TFS), and there is a slower fluctuation in the carrier's amplitude, called the envelope (ENV). Auditory nerve fibres signal both through the timing of their spikes (Joris and Yin, 1992). Fibres with CFs below roughly 4–5 kHz carry both cues; higher-CF fibres carry mainly the envelope (Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005).

Wiener-kernel analysis measures both in a single experiment. Play broadband Gaussian noise, record spikes, and cross-correlate the stimulus against the spike train. The first-order kernel describes the waveform the fibre responds to. The second-order kernel describes the envelope. Because the noise sits well above threshold, what comes out characterises coding under conditions resembling ordinary listening rather than at detection threshold (Eggermont et al., 1983; van Dijk et al., 1994; Lewis et al., 2002; Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005).

1. The first-order kernel: reverse correlation

Play a cell some noise, record its spikes, and ask a simple question: what did the noise look like just before each spike? Cut out the 10 ms before every spike, stack those snippets up, and average them. Any part of the waveform unrelated to firing averages towards zero. What survives is the filter the cell applies to its input. In auditory work this average is the reverse correlation, or revcor, and it goes back to the earliest systems-identification work on the auditory periphery (Eggermont et al., 1983).

R1(τ) = 1N ∑i=1N x(ti−τ) h1(τ) = R1(τ)A

Here x is the noise waveform, ti are the spike times, and A is the mean power of the noise. Dividing by A is what turns a raw average into a kernel with units, so that h1 can predict responses rather than merely describe them. Two numbers are usually taken from it: the frequency of the dominant component of its Fourier transform is the best frequency (BF) of TFS coding, and the peak of its Hilbert envelope is the amplitude of TFS coding (Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005; Henry et al., 2016).

Two things worth drawing out. The first is how fast it converges: one snippet is pure noise, ten is a hint, a few hundred is a recognisable filter. Nothing about the cell changed while you clicked — you were only collecting evidence. Real experiments accumulate 5000–10,000 driven spikes per fibre for exactly this reason (Henry et al., 2019). The second is what happens when you invert the noise. The estimate flips upside down, because this kernel tracks the shape of the waveform, sign and all. Hold on to that, because it is precisely the property the second-order kernel does not have.

The limitation of h1 is that it captures only what is linear in the response. A fibre that phase-locks poorly, as high-CF fibres do, yields a small or non-significant h1 even while it responds vigorously to the noise (Johnson, 1980; Heinz et al., 2001; Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005). Something is plainly driving those spikes, and reverse correlation on single time bins cannot see it.

2. The second-order kernel

The revcor asks about single time bins. The second-order kernel asks about pairs of them, and that one change is what lets a cell report the envelope of a sound rather than its waveform. The reason is worth stating plainly: a purely linear filter cannot represent the envelope of a zero-mean carrier at all, because the modulation averages out. Recovering an envelope requires an even nonlinearity — squaring or rectification — and a second-order term is exactly that.

R2(τ1,τ2) = 1N ∑i=1N x(ti−τ1) x(ti−τ2) h2(τ1,τ2) = R2(τ1,τ2) − φxx(τ2−τ1) 2A2

R2 is the average product of the stimulus at two lags before a spike. Subtracting the stimulus autocorrelation φxx removes the structure the noise has anyway, leaving what is attributable to the cell. Because Wiener kernels are orthogonalised against a Gaussian input, h1 and h2 can be estimated independently by cross-correlation, and truncating the series after the second term leaves the first unchanged — which is not true of the corresponding Volterra kernels (van Drongelen, 2010). A one-dimensional Fourier transform of h2 yields a spectro-temporal receptive field, readable as the mean stimulus spectrogram preceding a spike (Lewis and van Dijk, 2004).

2.1 Every square is a question about two time bins

The first-order kernel is a list of weights over time. The second-order kernel is a grid, and each square in it asks about a pair of time bins in the recent past: how much does this cell like it when those two bins agreed, both up or both down, versus disagreed? Two directions, two different questions. Across the diagonal is the gap between the two bins, and a gap is what frequency means, so that direction carries the carrier the cell is tuned to. Along the diagonal is how long ago both bins were, so that direction carries when. The stripes say 250 Hz; the fade past about 14 ms says the cell has no memory beyond that.

Real kernels contain two kinds of banding, and they mean different things. Bands running parallel to the diagonal, like the ones above, reflect phase locking to the envelope, and their spacing identifies the carrier band whose envelope the fibre follows. Bands running perpendicular to the diagonal instead reflect nonlinearity in the phase-locked response to the fine structure. A fibre showing both gives the kernel a checkerboard appearance (Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005; Henry et al., 2016). The two components separate cleanly in the two-dimensional Fourier transform of h2: envelope energy falls in the second and fourth quadrants and fine-structure energy in the first and third, so zeroing the first and third quadrants leaves a kernel driven primarily by envelope coding (Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005; Henry et al., 2016).

From there the usual step is eigenvector decomposition, which typically returns one or sometimes two pairs of significant eigenvectors in quadrature phase — the signature of a phase-insensitive energy detector. The Fourier transform of the first eigenvector gives the BF of envelope coding, the counterpart to what h1 gives for fine structure (Lewis et al., 2002; Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005). One caution when reading a published kernel: if the decomposition returns a single eigenvector that simply matches h1, the kernel is reporting curvature of the cell's output nonlinearity rather than a new feature, and there is no envelope claim to make.

2.2 The kernel never moves. The stimulus slides under it.

This is the step where the kernel stops being a picture and starts being a prediction. Multiply the kernel by the stimulus pairs happening right now, add up every square, and that sum is how hard the cell fires. Sweep through time and watch the left panel: it does not change, and it never will. Only the stimulus moves. What that sum computes is the second-order term of the Wiener series (van Drongelen, 2010).

y(t) = h0 + ∫h1(τ) x(t−τ) dτ + ∬h2(τ1,τ2) x(t−τ1) x(t−τ2) dτ1 dτ2

Three things to point out. The response peaks later than the loud patch, because the kernel's weight sits about 7 ms into the past. The second, quieter patch drives far less than half the response of the first, because envelope power goes as amplitude squared. And when the carrier stops matching the stripe spacing, coral squares start landing on disagreeing pairs, the contributions cancel, and the sum collapses — that cancellation is what carrier tuning looks like.

2.3 Fast and slow envelope following

These two cells are tuned to the same carrier. Their stripes are identical. The only difference is how far the kernel reaches along the diagonal, and that alone decides how quick an envelope each one can track. Narrow reach means a wide envelope passband; long reach means the cell averages over so much time that fast modulations wash out. Auditory nerve fibres are low-pass in the modulation domain, following slow envelopes faithfully and attenuating fast ones (Joris and Yin, 1992), and the diagonal extent of h2 is where that shows up.

This is the whole point of the diagonal direction. Nothing about carrier tuning changed between these two cells, and nothing about whether they code envelopes at all. Only the speed changed, and it changed because one kernel is punctate along the diagonal and the other is smeared. That is where an envelope cutoff lives.

Which matters for reading the published figures. When a paper shows the Fourier transform of h2 with frequency on one axis and time on the other, the frequency axis is carrier frequency, not modulation rate. A peak at 1.6 kHz says the fibre extracts the envelope of a 1.6 kHz band; it does not say the fibre responds to 1.6 kHz envelopes. The modulation tuning sits in the other, untransformed axis — the horizontal extent of the blob. Same kernel, perpendicular reading, different question.

In a real experiment

The chinchilla work these kernels are modelled on proceeds roughly as follows. A single auditory nerve fibre is isolated, a pure-tone threshold tuning curve establishes its CF, and a rate-level function for broadband noise fixes its noise threshold. Ten independent Gaussian noise tokens, 2.5 s long with a bandwidth of 16.5 kHz, are then presented repeatedly for several minutes at 10–20 dB above that threshold until 5000–10,000 driven spikes have accumulated. Correlations are computed with a sampling period of 0.02 ms out to a maximum lag of about 10 ms, or about 20 ms for fibres with CFs below 3 kHz, and spikes in the first 20 ms after stimulus onset are excluded to avoid onset transients (Recio-Spinoso et al., 2005; Henry et al., 2016, 2019).

Two practical points follow. The second-order kernel has on the order of half the square of the number of lags as free parameters, so it needs far more data than h1 for comparable reliability, which is why the spike counts are what they are. And significance has to be established rather than assumed: eigenvectors are kept only when their amplitude exceeds the noise floor by a stated margin, three standard deviations in this literature.

Why anyone measures this

In a healthy ear both cues are coded tonotopically: the BF of TFS coding and the BF of ENV coding both sit close to the fibre's CF, so each fibre reports on the frequency region it is meant to. Noise-induced hearing loss breaks this. Even mild loss introduces pathological coding of fine structure well below CF, and with moderate loss the envelope coding shifts downward too (Henry et al., 2016). Metabolic hearing loss, the age-related form caused by deterioration of the stria vascularis, produces the same kind of distortion but only at much greater threshold elevation; the difference between the two etiologies tracks the tip-to-tail ratio of the tuning curve rather than threshold as such (Henry et al., 2019).

Since speech perception depends on both cues (Lorenzi et al., 2006; Jørgensen et al., 2013), that dissociation is a candidate explanation for something clinically puzzling: why two people with nearly identical audiograms can differ substantially in how well they understand amplified speech. Which is a reasonable answer to a student who asks why anyone bothers averaging noise snippets in the first place.
